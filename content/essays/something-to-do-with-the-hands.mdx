There is a whetstone in the kitchen drawer, which is a strange place to keep one
and the only place I would ever use it. Twice a month, when the tomatoes start
giving up their skins instead of their flesh, I take it out and spend twenty
minutes making a dull thing sharp.

## The stone

Nothing about it is complicated, which is most of why it works. The stone is flat.
The angle is fifteen degrees, and then it is fifteen degrees again on the other
side, and then the burr comes up along the whole edge and you know you are done.
There is no decision to make and therefore no decision to regret.

The water helps. So does the sound, which is a light grinding at first and then,
right at the end, almost nothing.

## What the hands know

I used to think of this as time away from work — the small maintenance interval
between the things that count. Now I think it is one of the things that count, and
that the reason it feels restorative is not that it is a break from thinking but
that it is thinking with a shorter feedback loop. The edge either catches on my
thumbnail or it doesn't. No one has to tell me.

This is rare. Most of what I do in a day is judged later, by someone else, in a
meeting, in numbers I did not choose. The stone judges instantly and never
explains itself, and I have come to find that kindness rather than coldness.

## Doing it badly

My knives are not sharp the way a professional's are. I always stop a little
early, out of a small fear of ruining a good blade, and I have decided to keep
that fear rather than train it away. The point was never the knife.

What I want from the twenty minutes is the feeling of having done one thing
completely — started, finished, and put away. That feeling is available in the
kitchen twice a month, and almost nowhere else in my week.
